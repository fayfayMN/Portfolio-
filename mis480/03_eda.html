6) Demand-aware view: adjusting for stockouts¶

sale_amount only counts sales that actually happened. When an item was out of stock, no sales could be recorded, so sale_amount understates the real demand on those days. Chart 05 above shows observed sales dropping as stockout hours rise — but part of that drop is just this censoring, not customers wanting less.

Here is a simple correction. During business hours (6:00–22:00, which is 16 one-hour slots) the column stock_hour6_22_cnt tells us how many hours the item was out of stock. We assume the item would have sold at its normal in-stock rate during the hours it was unavailable, and scale the observed sales up by how much of the day it was actually available:

availability     = (16 - stockout_hours) / 16
estimated_demand = observed_sales / availability

Days where the item was out of stock for all 16 business hours give us nothing to scale from, so we leave those estimates blank.

In [10]:
# business-hour window used by stock_hour6_22_cnt: 6:00-22:00 = 16 one-hour slots
BUSINESS_HOURS = 16

# hours the item was actually on the shelf, and the share of the day it was available
df["instock_hours"] = BUSINESS_HOURS - df["stock_hour6_22_cnt"]
df["availability"] = df["instock_hours"] / BUSINESS_HOURS

# estimate the demand we would have seen if the item had been in stock all day.
# we assume the out-of-stock hours would have sold at the same rate as the in-stock
# hours, so we scale observed sales up by the availability rate. days that were out
# of stock the whole business day give us nothing to scale from, so we leave their
# estimate blank (NaN) instead of dividing by zero.
df["est_demand"] = np.nan
in_stock = df["availability"] > 0
df.loc[in_stock, "est_demand"] = df.loc[in_stock, "sale_amount"] / df.loc[in_stock, "availability"]

print("days out of stock all business hours (no estimate):", int((df["availability"] == 0).sum()))
print(f"mean observed sale_amount: {df['sale_amount'].mean():.2f}")
print(f"mean estimated demand:     {df['est_demand'].mean():.2f}")

What this shows¶

  • Observed sales fall as stockout hours rise, but estimated demand rises.
  • So the decline in chart 05 is mostly censoring, not weaker demand: the items that run out are the high-demand ones, and sale_amount cannot record sales once the shelf is empty.
  • Use sale_amount as observed sales and est_demand as a rough demand proxy. The proxy assumes out-of-stock hours would have sold at the same rate as in-stock hours that day — a simplification — and it says nothing about days that were out of stock the whole time (left blank).

9) Feature screening (Weeks 7 & 8)¶

Before modeling we screen the candidate predictors on two questions from the feature- selection lecture (Week 7):

  1. Relevance — how strongly does each predictor correlate with the target? (Pearson correlation, a dependence measure.)
  2. Redundancy — do predictors duplicate each other? We use the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); the Week 8 rule of thumb flags VIF > 5 (or |r| > 0.8).
In [14]:
# --- Feature screening: relevance and redundancy (Weeks 7 & 8) ---
predictors = ["discount", "stock_hour6_22_cnt", "holiday_flag", "activity_flag",
              "precpt", "avg_temperature", "avg_humidity", "avg_wind_level"]

# 1) Relevance: correlation of each predictor with the target.
#    We show it against BOTH the observed target and the stockout-adjusted demand.
relevance = df[predictors + ["sale_amount", "est_demand"]].corr()[["sale_amount", "est_demand"]]
print("Pearson correlation with the target:")
print(relevance.loc[predictors].round(3).to_string())

# 2) Redundancy: multicollinearity among the predictors.
#    VIF_i is the i-th diagonal entry of the inverse of the predictor correlation matrix.
corr_matrix = df[predictors].corr()
vif = np.diag(np.linalg.inv(corr_matrix.values))
vif_table = pd.Series(vif, index=predictors).round(2)
print("\nVariance Inflation Factor (VIF)   [rule of thumb: watch VIF > 5]:")
print(vif_table.to_string())

Reading the screen (Weeks 6-8):

  • Most relevant predictors: discount (r ~ -0.21 with sales: a lower price ratio, i.e. a deeper discount, goes with higher sales) and holiday_flag (r ~ +0.17). activity_flag and all weather variables are weak (|r| <= 0.07).
  • The censoring shows up again: stock_hour6_22_cnt correlates -0.08 with observed sales but +0.26 with estimated demand — the sign flips once we undo the stockout censoring. This is the Week 6 MNAR pattern: the "missing" demand is tied to its own size, because high-demand items are the ones that sell out.
  • Redundancy: every VIF is below 3 (highest ~2.8 for precipitation), well under the > 5 rule, so there is no multicollinearity problem among these predictors. The one exact redundancy is by construction — availability and stock_hour6_22_cnt carry identical information (r = -1.0), so a model keeps one, never both. discount and activity_flag are moderately related (r ~ -0.76, promotions usually come with price cuts) — worth watching, but under the |r| > 0.8 threshold.
  • Modeling implication (Week 8): numeric target -> Regression Trees + Multiple Linear Regression, consistent with the proposal; the Week 12-13 classifiers would only apply if we engineered a categorical label (e.g. "will this day stock out?").

10) Time-series view: trend and weekly seasonality (Weeks 3-4)¶

Weeks 3-4 decompose a time series into trend + seasonality + residual. Here we apply that to the aggregate daily-mean sales series (additive form: Y = T + S + residual), and show the day-of-week profile. This explains why lag_7 (same weekday last week) is such a strong predictor in notebook 04.

Note: 90 days can reveal a weekly cycle but not annual seasonality (proposal limitation).

In [15]:
# day-of-week profile
df["dow"] = df["dt"].dt.dayofweek
dow_means = df.groupby("dow")["sale_amount"].mean()
labels = ["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]

# additive decomposition of the aggregate daily-mean series
daily = df.groupby("dt")["sale_amount"].mean().sort_index()
trend = daily.rolling(7, center=True).mean()
detrended = daily - trend
seasonal = detrended.groupby(daily.index.dayofweek).transform("mean")   # weekly component
residual = daily - trend - seasonal
