Graduate Analytics Challenge · The Food Group

The finding that almost went out wrong

Forecasting food-shelf demand for a Minnesota hunger-relief network — and catching a confounder that reversed the headline conclusion before it reached the client.

The problem

The Food Group, a Minnesota hunger-relief network, needed two things: a demand forecast to plan distribution, and an honest read on where need goes unmet to guide where they site and route food. I entered the MinneMUDAC Graduate Division as an undergraduate.

The data

I built an end-to-end Python pipeline joining 8 operational and public datasets — Census ACS, the USDA Food Access Research Atlas, BLS, and SNAP administrative records among them — aligned across 87 Minnesota counties and 55 months. Most of the work was reconciling mismatched geographies and time grains so the joins actually meant something.

My approach

Forecasting. I evaluated 8 candidate models with walk-forward backtesting. A damped-trend exponential smoothing model out-forecast more complex SARIMA architectures and cut error ~33% below a persistence baseline — simpler, and more robust out of sample.

The confounder. The headline hypothesis was that the food-shelf network fills grocery “deserts.” A first cut said yes. But that comparison lumped in wealthy suburbs that would never host a shelf — so the gap might be income, not access. I stratified by neighborhood income and re-ran a Fisher's exact test: the advantage vanished (p = 0.76). The network tracks neighborhood poverty, not distance to a grocery store.

Policy shock. I quantified the SNAP benefit-cliff with an interrupted time series using Newey–West HAC robust standard errors (+22% visits, p < 0.001), and reported a null dose-response (ρ = +0.03) that bounded the causal claim instead of overstating it.

The result

Ranked #6 in Graduate Division prediction accuracy. More importantly, the confounder check turned a flashy-but-false headline into a defensible siting insight: coverage follows poverty, not access, so filling true access gaps needs a deliberate siting and mobile-distribution decision — it won't happen on its own.

What I'd do next

A food shelf's address is where food is handed out, not where its clients live — the real limit of the analysis. Next I'd start collecting client home ZIP to measure access properly, and test the ~29 unserved low-access tracts for mobile-route feasibility.

Why it matters

The best result here came from almost being wrong. A confounder I could have shipped would have sent the client in the wrong direction; the stratified re-test caught it. That — checking your own work before it becomes someone's decision — is the habit I'd bring to a team.

Does knowing tomorrow's promotions help you predict tomorrow's sales?

A full CRISP-DM study on 4.5M rows of retail data — and an honest answer to whether information available in advance beats simply using past sales.

The problem

Everyone forecasts retail sales from past sales. The real question: does information you know in advance — planned promotions, the calendar, weather, stockouts — actually improve next-day prediction beyond past sales alone? We framed every finding as an association and predictive contribution, never proof of causation.

The data

FreshRetailNet-50K — 4.5M rows, ~50,000 store-product series of daily sales with promotion/holiday flags, discounts, stockout hours, and weather. We worked a documented 100-series subset (2 cities × 5 stores × 10 products × 90 days = 9,000 complete rows) — a convenience sample, stated as such, not a random draw.

The censoring trap

The sharpest insight: when a product sells out, its observed sales fall (mean 1.14 → 0.46 as stockout hours rise) — but that's mostly censoring, not lost demand. We engineered a transparent proxy, est_demand = sales / availability, and the picture flipped (1.14 → 4.72). We flagged it as an MNAR (Missing Not At Random) pattern and kept observed sales as the primary target, with est_demand as a bounded proxy — not a silent assumption.

My approach

No leakage, by construction. Next-day forecasts used only past-sales lags and information known at forecast time; a strict chronological train/validation/test split, with preprocessing fit on training only.

Modeling. A log1p transform cut residual heteroscedasticity (0.53 → 0.24); I tuned Multiple Linear Regression and Random Forest and reported 95% prediction intervals that held at ~95% test coverage.

Honest evaluation. Scored once on the dataset's official future split — the genuine next 7 days — with lags built across the history→future boundary. A secondary classifier (“high-sales day?”) reached ROC-AUC ≈ 0.87, but binarizing discards information, so the regression stayed the main model.

The result

On genuinely unseen days, every model beat the naive baseline — so advance information gives a modest but real lift over past sales alone. That is the answer to the research question. The simple linear model held up best on RMSE; the forest's within-sample edge did not carry into the true future.

What I'd do next

A 90-day window can't show annual seasonality — I'd extend the history, move from a convenience sample to a random draw across the full chain, and monitor MAE/RMSE for drift to trigger scheduled retraining.

What I learned

Handling censoring honestly — naming the MNAR pattern and bounding the proxy instead of quietly imputing — mattered more than any model choice. And a model only earns trust when it beats the baseline on data it has genuinely never seen.
