Graduate Analytics Challenge · The Food Group

The finding that almost went out wrong

Forecasting food-shelf demand for a Minnesota hunger-relief network — and catching a confounder that reversed the headline conclusion before it reached the client.

The problem

The Food Group, a Minnesota hunger-relief network, needed two things: a demand forecast to plan distribution, and an honest read on where need goes unmet to guide where they site and route food. I entered the MinneMUDAC Graduate Division as an undergraduate.

The data

I built an end-to-end Python pipeline joining 8 operational and public datasets — Census ACS, the USDA Food Access Research Atlas, BLS, and SNAP administrative records among them — aligned across 87 Minnesota counties and 55 months. Most of the work was reconciling mismatched geographies and time grains so the joins actually meant something.

My approach

Forecasting. I evaluated 8 candidate models with walk-forward backtesting. A damped-trend exponential smoothing model out-forecast more complex SARIMA architectures and cut error ~33% below a persistence baseline — simpler, and more robust out of sample.

The confounder. The headline hypothesis was that the food-shelf network fills grocery “deserts.” A first cut said yes. But that comparison lumped in wealthy suburbs that would never host a shelf — so the gap might be income, not access. I stratified by neighborhood income and re-ran a Fisher's exact test: the advantage vanished (p = 0.76). The network tracks neighborhood poverty, not distance to a grocery store.

Policy shock. I quantified the SNAP benefit-cliff with an interrupted time series using Newey–West HAC robust standard errors (+22% visits, p < 0.001), and reported a null dose-response (ρ = +0.03) that bounded the causal claim instead of overstating it.

The result

Ranked #6 in Graduate Division prediction accuracy. More importantly, the confounder check turned a flashy-but-false headline into a defensible siting insight: coverage follows poverty, not access, so filling true access gaps needs a deliberate siting and mobile-distribution decision — it won't happen on its own.

What I'd do next

A food shelf's address is where food is handed out, not where its clients live — the real limit of the analysis. Next I'd start collecting client home ZIP to measure access properly, and test the ~29 unserved low-access tracts for mobile-route feasibility.

Why it matters

The best result here came from almost being wrong. A confounder I could have shipped would have sent the client in the wrong direction; the stratified re-test caught it. That — checking your own work before it becomes someone's decision — is the habit I'd bring to a team.

Finding the next sales call in millions of barcodes

Turning raw UPC transaction data into a ranked list of which supplier customers to follow up with — by finding where they already sell to retailers they aren't yet connected to.

The problem

SPS Commerce runs the largest retail business network in the industry, connecting suppliers and retailers through EDI. The business question: which supplier customers should the team follow up with first? The signal we went after — a supplier already selling a product at retailers they are not yet connected to through SPS. That connection gap is a concrete growth opportunity.

The data

Item-level UPC transaction records (daily files, millions of rows) joined to customer details through an organization-ID key. The quiet trap: UPCs are 12-digit codes with leading zeros and the org IDs are enormous integers — read either as a number and the data silently corrupts. Everything had to be read as strings, and the cleaned outputs never opened in Excel, which would reformat the IDs on contact.

My approach

ETL. Unzip and combine the raw daily files, keep only valid 12-digit UPCs, clean and de-duplicate the item records.

Join + EDA. Attach customer attributes — industry, revenue band, tenure, existing SPS connection count — to each item via the seller organization ID.

Enrichment. An external API lookup to find which retailers actually carry each barcode in the market.

Gap analysis + prioritization. Compare “retailers who sell this supplier's products” against “retailers they're connected to via SPS.” The difference is the opportunity. I ranked customers by that gap and built an ML-assisted prioritizer to spend the limited API budget on the highest-value targets first, delivering a ranked sales-target list in Excel / Power BI.

The result

A reproducible pipeline — ordered notebooks plus standalone scripts for unattended runs — that takes raw barcode data and hands the business team a prioritized follow-up list instead of a spreadsheet dump. Each step writes the file the next one reads, so the whole thing reruns on fresh data.

What I'd do next

Scale the API enrichment with caching and rate-limit handling, add fuzzy product matching for barcodes the lookup misses, and automate a refresh so the target list regenerates as new transaction data lands.

What I learned

Two things. First, data-type discipline at the boundary — leading zeros, oversized IDs — is the difference between a correct pipeline and silent, invisible data loss. Second, an analysis is only worth anything if it maps to a decision the business actually makes: here, who to call next.
